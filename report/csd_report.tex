%% =================== ABSTRACT ======================================
\begin{abstract}
Village ponds are among the cheapest ways to harvest rainwater in rural India, but deciding
where to dig one, and how big to make it, normally needs a field survey and a hydrologist. We
present a web application that does this preliminary planning from free public data. Given a
village name or a map click, the server builds a 20\,m elevation model from global terrain
tiles (or from an uploaded KML contour map), routes surface flow with Priority-Flood
depression filling and the D8 algorithm, and identifies land available for excavation by
combining an OpenCV classifier on satellite imagery with OpenStreetMap exclusions and optional
government parcels. Candidate sites are scored on flow accumulation, slope, concavity and land
suitability, and a ranking algorithm returns up to five sites whose catchments are
hydrologically independent. Twenty years of daily CHIRPS rainfall drive a daily SCS Curve
Number runoff model, and a pond is sized to half of the 75\,\%-dependable annual runoff. The
system is a FastAPI monolith with an SQLite store, multi-level caching and fallback chains for
every external service, behind a Leaflet front end. Thirty-five tests pass; CHIRPS
rainfall is within 8.9\,\% of IMD station normals on average, and the land classifier is
92\,\% accurate ($\kappa=0.85$) on 100 blind-labelled cells. For Hiware Bazar (Maharashtra)
the system recommends five ponds storing 121\,155\,m$^3$ in total. A first analysis takes
about 44\,s, dominated by the rainfall download; a cached analysis takes 1.3\,s, and eight
concurrent cached analyses complete in 1.3\,s each.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Most of India's net sown area is rain-fed, and most of its rain falls in the four months of
the south-west monsoon. Small surface storages --- farm ponds, village tanks and the
\emph{Amrit Sarovars} built under national programmes~\cite{amritsarovar} --- hold this water
back for irrigation, livestock and groundwater recharge. A pond is only useful, however, if
three early decisions are right: it must sit on a drainage line that actually receives runoff
and on land the village is allowed to dig; its catchment must deliver enough water even in a
dry year; and its volume must match that inflow, since a pond much larger than its dependable
inflow rarely fills and one much smaller wastes the catchment.

The goal of this assignment is a web application that analyses terrain and rainfall to
recommend suitable pond locations and to estimate catchment area, rainfall, runoff, pond depth
and storage capacity, all shown on an interactive map. Section~\ref{sec:requirements} states
the requirements, Section~\ref{sec:architecture} the architecture,
Section~\ref{sec:methodology} the algorithms and Section~\ref{sec:implementation} the
implementation. Section~\ref{sec:csd-themes} maps the design to Computer System Design themes,
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results and measured performance, and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses limitations.

\subsection{Motivation}
Choosing a pond site by hand is hard for four reasons. First, terrain is subtle: on the
gently sloping Deccan plateau a drainage line can be invisible on the ground, and a few metres
of relief decide where water collects. Second, rainfall data is scattered: IMD station records
are sparse, gridded products differ widely, and a village administrator rarely knows the
dependable (dry-year) rainfall that should govern design. Third, land ownership records are
not openly available, so finding land that is both free and hydrologically useful needs local
knowledge. Fourth, sizing requires a runoff model, which is beyond most panchayat staff. An
automated tool that turns a village name into a short, quantified list of candidate sites lets
an engineer spend field time verifying a handful of good options instead of searching a whole
village.

\subsection{Scope of the Project}
The system \textbf{does}: find a village by name or map click; build a terrain model from an
elevation service or an uploaded KML/KMZ contour map; display satellite imagery, contours,
drainage and land cover; identify land available for excavation; recommend up to five ranked
pond sites with independent catchments (or evaluate a site chosen by the user); delineate each
catchment; retrieve 5--40 years of daily rainfall; estimate runoff; recommend pond dimensions,
storage, earthwork and water losses; and save, list and export analyses.

The system \textbf{does not}: perform structural or geotechnical design of embankments,
inlets or spillways; determine legal land ownership (it can only use parcels supplied by the
administrator); model groundwater, soil infiltration measurements or water quality; estimate
costs; or replace a field survey. It is a pre-feasibility tool.

\subsection{Non-Functional Requirements}
\begin{description}
  \item[Performance] A first analysis of a new village should finish within about one minute,
    and a repeat analysis within 5\,s. Lightweight endpoints (health, cached rainfall,
    geocoding) should answer in under 50\,ms.
  \item[Scalability] One instance should serve a small team of planners --- up to about ten
    concurrent analyses --- without degrading results. The server is stateless apart from the
    database and caches, so it can be scaled horizontally later.
  \item[Availability] The failure of any single external data service must degrade a result,
    not abort it, and the degradation must be reported to the user in the response.
  \item[Usability] A non-specialist must be able to run an analysis with only a village name.
    The interface must work with keyboard and screen readers and meet WCAG AA contrast, and
    target current desktop browsers (Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge).
  \item[Security] All input is validated (ranges, enumerations, a 25\,MB upload limit); no
    secret is needed to run the system; external services are called only from the server.
  \item[Cost and portability] Only free, key-less public data; installable from pip wheels
    without GIS server software; deployable in a container on free hosting.
  \item[Transparency] Every output must be traceable: the response carries data sources,
    methods, parameters and per-stage timings.
\end{description}

\section{System Architecture and High-Level Design}
\label{sec:architecture}
The system is a three-tier web application (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The
\emph{client} is a static single-page application (HTML, CSS, JavaScript with Leaflet and
Chart.js). The \emph{application server} is a FastAPI process that contains all analysis
logic in one analysis pipeline. The \emph{persistence tier} is an SQLite database (saved
analyses and an API-response cache) plus a disk cache of map tiles. The \emph{external API
layer} consists of seven free public services, which only the server calls; this lets the
server set proper User-Agent headers, cache responses and stay within fair-use limits.

A request flows as follows. The browser sends \code{POST /api/analyze} with a location. The
server downloads terrain tiles (or reads them from the tile cache) and builds a DEM; fetches
satellite imagery and OpenStreetMap features to classify land; scores and ranks sites; fetches
the rainfall series (or reads it from \code{api\_cache}); evaluates catchment, runoff and pond
design for every candidate; saves the result in SQLite; and returns one JSON document with
GeoJSON layers and PNG overlays, which the client draws on the map.

\subsection{Technology Stack}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Language and server:} Python 3.12+ with FastAPI 0.115 and Uvicorn; Pydantic 2
    for request/response models, which also generate the OpenAPI documentation at
    \code{/docs}.
  \item \textbf{Numerical and image processing:} NumPy, SciPy (Delaunay interpolation) and
    OpenCV (tile decoding, resampling with \code{cv2.remap}, contour tracing, morphology,
    classification, rasterisation).
  \item \textbf{Database:} SQLite through the Python standard library.
  \item \textbf{Frontend:} plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript, Leaflet 1.9 with Leaflet.draw for maps
    and parcels, and Chart.js 4 for charts.
  \item \textbf{Data services:} AWS Terrain Tiles (elevation, SRTM/Copernicus $\sim$30\,m) with
    Open-Meteo elevation as fallback; Esri World Imagery; OpenStreetMap Overpass and
    Nominatim; CHIRPS v2.0 rainfall via NASA SERVIR ClimateSERV, with Open-Meteo (ERA5) and
    NASA POWER as fallbacks; Open-Meteo or NASA POWER for evapotranspiration.
  \item \textbf{Deployment:} Docker image and a Render blueprint (\code{render.yaml}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Deviations from the suggested stack.} The brief suggests IMD for rainfall. IMD
gridded rainfall is distributed as bulk files rather than through a query API, so CHIRPS was
chosen after validating three datasets against IMD station normals
(Section~\ref{sec:results}): NASA POWER was off by up to $+92\,\%$ in the rain shadow, CHIRPS
by 8.9\,\% on average. We did not use GDAL or a GIS server: the required algorithms are small
enough to implement directly in NumPy and OpenCV, which keeps the system installable from pip
wheels on free hosting. SQLite replaces a client--server database because the data is a small
document store with a single writer process.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Terrain and Elevation Analysis}
For a village centre and radius $R$ (default 2\,km) the server lays out an $N\times N$ grid
(default $N=200$, cell size $\Delta=20$\,m) in a local equirectangular projection. AWS Terrain
Tiles encode elevation in the RGB bytes of 256$\times$256 Web-Mercator PNG tiles as
$z = 256R + G + B/256 - 32768$\,m. The tiles covering the grid are downloaded in parallel,
decoded with OpenCV and stitched into a mosaic, which is then sampled at every cell centre
with \code{cv2.remap} (bilinear). Decoding happens \emph{before} resampling, because
interpolating the packed bytes would mix the high and low bytes of neighbouring pixels and
produce errors of hundreds of metres. If the tile service fails, a 40$\times$40 lattice is
queried from the Open-Meteo elevation API and interpolated bicubically.

For an uploaded KML/KMZ map, the parser detects each contour's elevation from coordinate $z$
values, \code{ExtendedData}, the placemark name or the folder name (the first strategy that
covers 90\,\% of lines with varying values wins), and the contour vertices are interpolated
linearly over their Delaunay triangulation.

Contours for display are traced from the DEM with \code{cv2.findContours} at an automatic
interval, with major and minor lines; slope and plan curvature come from finite differences.
Coastal areas need one more step: the terrain tiles report the sea as a flat 0\,m surface with
bathymetry further out, so connected low areas containing enough sea-surface or bathymetry
cells are marked as sea and excluded, while low-lying inland land (for example the polders of
Kuttanad, below sea level) is kept.

\subsection{Catchment Area Delineation}
Catchments are derived from the DEM with standard flow-routing algorithms:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Depression filling.} Priority-Flood~\cite{barnes2014} seeds a priority queue
    with the edge cells, repeatedly pops the lowest cell and raises each unvisited neighbour to
    at least its elevation plus $10^{-4}$\,m, so every cell has a downhill path to the edge
    ($O(n\log n)$).
  \item \textbf{Flow direction (D8).} Each cell drains to the neighbour with the steepest
    distance-weighted drop~\cite{ocallaghan1984}, computed as eight vectorised array shifts
    ($O(n)$).
  \item \textbf{Flow accumulation.} Cells are visited in decreasing filled elevation, each
    adding its count to its downstream neighbour ($O(n\log n)$ for the sort).
  \item \textbf{Catchment.} Breadth-first traversal of the reversed D8 graph from the pond
    cell gives the catchment mask ($O(n)$). Its boundary is traced along cell edges into a
    GeoJSON polygon whose area equals the cell count times $\Delta^2$.
  \item \textbf{Edge check.} Cells whose catchment may extend beyond the map edge are
    flagged; in village mode they are excluded from siting so that no catchment is truncated.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Site selection.} Candidate cells are available-land cells on the drainage network
(top 8\,\% of accumulation, relaxed stepwise if available land does not reach it). Each is
scored
\[
S = 0.75\,(0.45\,\hat A + 0.35\,(1-\hat\theta) + 0.20\,\hat\kappa) + 0.25\,L,
\]
where $\hat A$ is normalised log-accumulation, $\hat\theta$ normalised slope, $\hat\kappa$
normalised concavity and $L$ the land score. Sites are then accepted best-first only if they
are at least 250\,m from, and neither upstream nor downstream of, every accepted site, so the
inflows of the (up to five) ponds can be added without double counting.

\paragraph{Available land.} Esri imagery at 5\,m is classified per pixel: vegetation by the
Green--Red Vegetation Index $(G-R)/(G+R)$ with an Otsu threshold~\cite{otsu1979} clamped to
$[0,0.10]$; built-up as dense clusters of bright, highly textured pixels; water as dark,
blue-dominant smooth pixels; the rest as open land. OpenStreetMap buildings (+20\,m buffer),
roads (+4--30\,m), railways, rivers, water bodies, forest, settlements and protected areas are
excluded; grassland, scrub or common-land tags are preferred; parcels drawn by the
administrator, if any, restrict siting to them; ground steeper than 8\textdegree{} is
excluded.

\paragraph{Assumptions and limitations.} D8 routes all flow to one neighbour and cannot model
divergent flow; the $\sim$30\,m source DEM cannot see bunds or drains narrower than about
30\,m; and land availability is inferred, not legal ownership.

\subsection{Rainfall Data Integration}
Daily precipitation is taken from CHIRPS v2.0~\cite{funk2015} (0.05\textdegree{},
satellite--gauge blend) through the ClimateSERV API, downloaded as two ten-year chunks in
parallel. If CHIRPS fails the system falls back to Open-Meteo's ERA5
reanalysis~\cite{hersbach2020}, then to NASA POWER. Reference evapotranspiration comes from
Open-Meteo (FAO-56 Penman--Monteith) or, from NASA POWER temperatures, by Hargreaves'
equation~\cite{allen1998}. The default record is the last 20 complete years (2006--2025),
configurable from 5 to 40. If the administrator knows the local IMD normal, the daily series is
scaled to it, preserving the storm pattern while removing bias.

From the daily series the system computes: mean, median, standard deviation and coefficient of
variation of annual totals; the 75\,\%-dependable rainfall (the 25th percentile of annual
totals, equalled or exceeded three years in four); the driest and wettest years; rainy days
($\geq$2.5\,mm, the IMD definition); the heaviest day; the monsoon (June--September) share;
monthly climatology; and annual and dry-season (October--May) evapotranspiration.

\paragraph{Runoff and pond design.} Runoff is simulated for every day with the SCS Curve
Number method~\cite{tr55}: $S=25400/CN-254$, $I_a=0.2S$, $Q=(P-I_a)^2/(P-I_a+S)$ for $P>I_a$.
Curve numbers come from each land-cover class for the chosen soil group, are adjusted daily
to dry or wet antecedent conditions from the previous five days' rain~\cite{hawkins1985}, and
runoff is area-weighted over the catchment's land cover. The pond is a rectangular excavated
frustum (side slope 1.5:1, length 1.5$\times$ width, 0.5\,m freeboard) holding half of the
75\,\%-dependable annual runoff, so it fills about twice even in a dry year. Depth starts at
3\,m (MGNREGA/Amrit Sarovar practice) and increases to at most 4.5\,m if the footprint does
not fit on 80\,\% of the connected available land; volume is computed with the prismoidal
formula. The design reports earthwork, dry-season evaporation and seepage.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
The back end is about 3\,400 lines of Python in fifteen modules; the front end about 1\,200
lines of HTML, CSS and JavaScript. \code{main.py} handles only HTTP; \code{pipeline.py}
orchestrates the stages; each analysis module is a function of arrays and parameters that can
be tested in isolation; network and disk I/O are confined to \code{tiles.py},
\code{rainfall.py}, \code{landcover.py}, \code{geocode.py} and \code{db.py}.

\subsection{Backend and API Design}
The API is REST over JSON (Table~\ref{tab:api}). The full schema is generated from the
Pydantic models and served at \code{/docs}.

\paragraph{Authentication.} There is none: the system is a single-tenant planning tool that
handles only public data, so every user may run and view analyses. Section~\ref{sec:csd-themes}
discusses what adding it would involve.

\paragraph{Errors.} All errors share one JSON shape, \code{\{"status": "error", "detail":
"..."\}}: 400 for an empty upload, 413 for an oversized one, 422 for invalid input (including
a location in the open sea) and 502 when a required upstream service is down. Third-party
failures are handled at three levels: (i) every source has a fallback --- terrain tiles fall
back to the Open-Meteo elevation API, and rainfall falls through CHIRPS, Open-Meteo and NASA
POWER, with transient network errors retried with linear back-off; (ii) optional evidence
(imagery, OpenStreetMap) may fail without aborting the analysis, and the loss is added to the
response's \code{warnings}; (iii) if all rainfall sources fail, the rational method is applied
to a fallback rainfall value, again with a warning.

\subsection{Frontend and Visualization}
The front end (Figure~\ref{fig:ui}) is a single page with \emph{Village}, \emph{Contour file}
and \emph{History} tabs over a Leaflet map. The Village tab offers search, a preview of the
analysis square, parcel drawing and GeoJSON upload with Leaflet.draw, an optional manual pond
pin and advanced parameters (soil group, rainfall dataset and normal, storage fraction, depth
and slope limits). While an analysis runs, a staged progress overlay covers the map. Results
appear as a headline storage card and four key figures, a ranked candidate table mirrored by
numbered map markers (selecting a candidate redraws everything for it), and \emph{Pond},
\emph{Rainfall}, \emph{Runoff} and \emph{Site \& land} sub-tabs with a pond cross-section and
Chart.js charts. Map layers --- contours, drainage, catchment, available land, land-cover and
relief rasters, pond footprints --- can be toggled. Results export as JSON or GeoJSON and print
through a dedicated stylesheet. Accessibility work includes ARIA tab semantics, keyboard
operation, visible focus, a skip link and AA text contrast.

\subsection{Database and Storage}
SQLite holds two tables. \code{analyses} stores every completed analysis twice: a compact
summary (\code{summary\_json}) for the history list and the full response
(\code{result\_json}) for exact re-display, together with the village, mode, the top site's
coordinates and a UTC timestamp; an index on \code{created\_at} serves the newest-first history
query, and single analyses are fetched by primary key. \code{api\_cache} is a key--value table
(\code{key}, \code{created\_at}, JSON \code{payload}) that memoises external responses with a
time-to-live: rainfall series for 30 days (keyed by source, location rounded to 0.01\textdegree{}
and years), OpenStreetMap features for 7 days (keyed by bounding box) and geocoding for 7 days.
Map tiles are cached as files keyed by source, zoom and tile coordinates. Spatial data
(catchments, footprints, land polygons) is stored as GeoJSON inside the result document rather
than in spatial tables, because it is only ever read back as a whole. All access goes through
\code{db.py}, which serialises writes with a lock.

\paragraph{Themes not implemented.} \emph{Load balancing}: one Uvicorn process serves the
expected load (a few planners); the bottleneck is external APIs, not CPU, and SQLite favours a
single writer process. Scaling out would mean running several workers behind a reverse proxy
such as Nginx and moving the database and caches to PostgreSQL and Redis. \emph{Authentication
and authorisation}: all data is public and the tool is single-tenant; a multi-panchayat
deployment would need login (e.g.\ OAuth) and per-village ownership of saved analyses.
\emph{Version control / CI-CD}: the code is kept in a Git repository (Appendix~\ref{app:repo});
there is no CI pipeline yet --- running the unit tests in GitHub Actions on every push would be
the first step.

The three most significant decisions were the following. \textbf{(1) Caching plus resilience
around external APIs.} Every slow or fragile dependency sits behind a cache and a fallback.
Load testing showed why both are needed: with four concurrent analyses, uncached OpenStreetMap
queries exceeded Overpass's per-IP limit, each request waited about 44\,s and then lost all its
OpenStreetMap exclusions. We added a 7-day cache, serialised the calls and added a
circuit-breaker cool-down, after which a failing Overpass costs one timeout instead of one per
request. \textbf{(2) A modular monolith instead of microservices.} The stages pass large arrays
between them, the team is one person and the hosting is a single free instance; a monolith
with strict module boundaries gives the testability of services without their operational
cost. \textbf{(3) Choosing data by measurement.} The rainfall source was initially Open-Meteo
with NASA POWER as fallback; validation against IMD normals showed errors up to 92\,\%, so
CHIRPS became the default and the others fallbacks --- an architectural decision driven by
data quality rather than convenience.

\section{Results and Evaluation}
\label{sec:results}
\paragraph{Case study: Hiware Bazar.} Hiware Bazar (Ahilyanagar district, Maharashtra) is a
model watershed village on the semi-arid Deccan plateau. We analysed a 4\,km$\times$4\,km area
centred at 19.068\textdegree{}N, 74.601\textdegree{}E with soil group C and default
parameters. The figures below are from a run on 27 September 2026; because imagery,
OpenStreetMap and CHIRPS are live sources, later runs can differ slightly (a rerun on
2 October, after one road was removed from OpenStreetMap, reordered the two nearly equal
leading sites).

The DEM spans 692--875\,m. After exclusions, 76\,\% of the area (1\,219\,ha) is suitable for
excavation. CHIRPS gives a mean annual rainfall of 713\,mm for 2006--2025, a 75\,\%-dependable
rainfall of 599\,mm and a coefficient of variation of 23\,\% (from 373\,mm in 2018 to 1\,063\,mm
in 2020); 79\,\% falls in June--September (Figure~\ref{fig:results}). The ranking algorithm
found five independent sites, all on open land with slopes of 0.1--1.6\textdegree{}
(Table~\ref{tab:cands}).

\paragraph{Comparison with reference data.} Each component was checked against independent
references (Table~\ref{tab:validation}). Against IMD 1991--2020 normals at six observatories,
CHIRPS has a mean absolute error of 8.9\,\% (Ahmednagar, the nearest station to Hiware Bazar,

elasticity of about three with respect to rainfall ($\pm$10\,\% rain gives $-30$/$+31\,\%$
runoff) and varies by a factor of 4.7 between soil groups A and D, so rainfall and soil group
dominate the uncertainty.

A first analysis is dominated by the rainfall download (about 35\,s for two parallel ten-year

accumulation is a single sorted pass.

\paragraph{Behaviour under load.} Load testing exposed a real problem. With the original code,
four concurrent cached analyses each took 45\,s instead of 1.3\,s and all of them returned
without OpenStreetMap data: OpenStreetMap queries were not cached, the public Overpass server
admits only about two queries per IP at a time, and the others timed out. We added a 7-day
cache, serialised Overpass calls and a 120\,s cool-down after a failure. During our tests
Overpass then refused connections from our network altogether --- a temporary block, most likely
triggered by the load test itself --- which let us test the failure path: a batch of four
concurrent requests shared a single 36\,s timeout (previously up to 293\,s for the last request),
and the following eight concurrent requests completed in 1.3\,s each, with the missing
OpenStreetMap data reported in their warnings. Because NumPy and OpenCV release Python's global
interpreter lock, eight concurrent analyses took no longer than one: the CPU is not the
bottleneck; external APIs are. The successful-cache path is covered by unit tests but could
not be load-tested live while Overpass blocked our network.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{What worked well.} Building everything on one in-memory DEM made the two input modes
share all downstream code. Validating data sources early changed the design for the better
(the switch to CHIRPS). Returning methods, timings and warnings with every result made
failures visible instead of silent, and end-to-end testing found two bugs that unit tests
missed.

\paragraph{What did not.} The original RGB water rule (blue brighter than red and green) does
not recognise the turbid, green-tinted water typical of Indian coasts and reservoirs; before we
fixed it, the system recommended ponds offshore for a Konkan coastal village and even on the
Pacific seabed. Sea is now excluded using the elevation data, but inland water still relies on
OpenStreetMap. The dependence on live public services makes results drift as those services
change, and some services rate-limit aggressively.

\paragraph{Data quality and assumptions.} The $\sim$30\,m DEM cannot resolve field bunds,
drains or road embankments, which can redirect flow on the ground. CHIRPS underestimates
deserts ($-28\,\%$ at Jaisalmer). Soil group is a user input and is the most influential
parameter after rainfall. The SCS-CN method with TR-55 curve numbers was developed for US
catchments; its use in India is standard in watershed manuals but not locally calibrated.
Land ownership is inferred from tags and imagery, never certain, and groundwater depth and
rock are ignored when choosing depth. All recommendations therefore need field verification.
